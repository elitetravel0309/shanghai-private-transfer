Shanghai Food Tour & Restaurant Transfer Guide 2026

September 7, 2026 13 min read By Shanghai Private Transfer

Shanghai is one of the world's greatest food cities — a dazzling culinary melting pot where classic Shanghainese cuisine, regional Chinese specialties, and world-class international restaurants coexist. From soup dumplings (xiaolongbao) in a historic shikumen alley to Michelin-starred fine dining in a gleaming skyscraper, Shanghai offers an unforgettable food journey for every palate and budget.

But Shanghai's best restaurants are spread across multiple neighborhoods — from the historic alleys of the Old City to the trendy enclaves of Xintiandi and the former French Concession. Navigating between them by taxi or metro can be time-consuming and stressful, especially after a long meal or when carrying shopping bags.

In this complete guide, we cover the best Shanghai restaurants by cuisine and neighborhood, suggested food tour itineraries, and why a private car with driver is the perfect way to experience Shanghai's culinary scene.

Shanghai Food Neighborhoods Guide

1. The Old City (Nanshi) & Yu Garden Area

  • Vibe: Historic, traditional, atmospheric — the oldest part of Shanghai
  • Best for: Classic Shanghainese street food, traditional snacks, tea houses
  • Must-try dishes: Nanxiang xiaolongbao (soup dumplings), shengjianbao (pan-fried pork buns), scallion oil pancakes, red bean paste cakes, osmanthus candy
  • Notable restaurants: Nanxiang Mantou Dian (Yu Garden), Lu Bo Lang Restaurant, Huxinting Tea House
  • Tip: Arrive before 10 AM to avoid the worst crowds at Yu Garden. The surrounding old lanes have better, cheaper street food than the touristy Yu Garden Bazaar itself.

2. Former French Concession (Xuhui / Huangpu)

  • Vibe: Tree-lined streets, European architecture, trendy cafes, boutique shops
  • Best for: International cuisine, trendy brunch, coffee culture, fine dining
  • Must-try dishes: Shanghai-style French cuisine, artisanal coffee, craft beer, fusion dishes, bakery pastries
  • Notable restaurants: Mr & Mrs Bund, Ultraviolet (3 Michelin stars), Fu He Hui (vegetarian), Yongfoo Elite, Commune Social
  • Tip: Wander along Yongkang Road, Anfu Road, and Wukang Road for some of Shanghai's best cafes, bakeries, and boutique shops. This area is perfect for a leisurely afternoon food crawl.

3. Xintiandi

  • Vibe: Restored shikumen (stone-gate) houses, upscale dining, nightlife
  • Best for: Upscale Chinese and international restaurants, rooftop bars, fine dining
  • Must-try dishes: High-end Shanghainese cuisine, Peking duck, Japanese omakase, wine bars
  • Notable restaurants: Hakkasan, T8, Shintori, The Fat Olive, Brownstone
  • Tip: Xintiandi is more expensive than other neighborhoods but offers a beautiful setting for a special dinner. Arrive early to explore the boutique shops before your meal.

4. Lujiazui (Pudong)

  • Vibe: Modern skyscrapers, river views, luxury hotels, high-end malls
  • Best for: Fine dining with skyline views, hotel restaurants, international cuisine
  • Must-try dishes: Cantonese dim sum, Japanese sushi, Italian fine dining, buffet brunches
  • Notable restaurants: Jin Xuan (The Ritz-Carlton), 8½ Otto e Mezzo Bombana, Nadaman, M on the Bund (across the river)
  • Tip: Book a window table at a Lujiazui restaurant for dinner to enjoy the illuminated Shanghai skyline. The view from the 50th+ floor is spectacular.

5. The Bund & North Bund

  • Vibe: Historic colonial buildings, river views, iconic skyline backdrop
  • Best for: Fine dining with Pudong skyline views, classic European cuisine, rooftop bars
  • Must-try dishes: Modern European, steak and seafood, cocktails with a view
  • Notable restaurants: M on the Bund, Jean-Georges, The Chop Chop Club, Bar Rouge
  • Tip: The Bund is perfect for a special occasion dinner. Arrive 30 minutes early to walk along the Bund promenade and enjoy the view of the Pudong skyline before dinner.

6. Food Markets & Night Markets

  • Qipu Road Food Street: Affordable local snacks, popular with locals
  • Shouning Road Night Market: Late-night seafood stalls, crayfish, barbecue — open until 2–3 AM
  • Zhaozhou Road Food Street: Trendy casual restaurants, craft beer, international street food
  • Tianzifang: Artistic alleyways with cafes, boutique shops, and casual eateries
  • Tip: Night markets are best visited after 7 PM. A private car is essential for safe transportation back to your hotel after a late-night food adventure.

Tips for a Great Shanghai Food Tour

  1. Book a full-day private car — Shanghai's best restaurants are spread across multiple neighborhoods. A full-day car (8–10 hours) lets you explore without worrying about transportation between meals.
  2. Plan your itinerary by neighborhood — Group restaurants by area to minimize travel time: Old City in the morning, French Concession in the afternoon, The Bund/Xintiandi for dinner.
  3. Make reservations in advance — Popular and high-end restaurants fill up quickly, especially on weekends. Book 1–2 weeks in advance for fine dining, 2–3 days for popular casual spots.
  4. Ask your driver for recommendations — Local drivers know the best hidden gems, the most authentic local spots, and can help avoid tourist traps. They can also help with restaurant reservations in Chinese.
  5. Wear comfortable shoes — Even with a car, you'll do a lot of walking between restaurants, in food markets, and exploring neighborhoods.
  6. Bring cash — While most restaurants accept mobile payment (Alipay/WeChat), some smaller street food stalls and traditional shops may only accept cash. Carry some Chinese Yuan (¥) for these spots.
  7. Try the seasonal specialties — Shanghai cuisine is highly seasonal. Autumn (September–November) is hairy crab season; spring (March–May) features fresh bamboo shoots and river fish; summer offers cold noodles and shaved ice desserts.
  8. Don't overeat at one meal — Pace yourself! Shanghai food tours are about sampling many dishes across multiple restaurants. Share dishes family-style so you can try more without getting too full.
  9. Stay hydrated — Shanghai can be hot and humid, especially in summer. Drink plenty of water between meals. Your driver can stop at convenience stores for drinks.
  10. Book your return transfer for after dinner — After a long day of eating and drinking, the last thing you want is to hail a taxi or navigate the metro. Pre-book your return transfer for a safe, comfortable ride back to your hotel.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the best time of year for a Shanghai food tour?

A: Shanghai is a great food destination year-round, but spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) offer the most comfortable weather for exploring neighborhoods and food markets. Autumn is especially popular for hairy crab season. Summer can be hot and humid, while winter is cold but less crowded.

Q: Can you recommend a food guide or interpreter?

A: While our drivers speak English and can provide local recommendations, we can also arrange a professional English-speaking food guide for an additional fee (typically ¥500–¥800/day). A food guide can provide detailed explanations of dishes, help with ordering, and share cultural context. Contact us in advance to arrange a food guide.

Q: Can you help with restaurant reservations?

A: Yes! Our English-speaking team can help make restaurant reservations at most Shanghai restaurants. Simply provide us with your restaurant preferences, date, and time, and we'll handle the booking. For very popular or Michelin-starred restaurants, we recommend booking 1–2 weeks in advance.

Q: Is Shanghai food spicy?

A: Traditional Shanghainese cuisine is generally not spicy — it's characterized by sweet and savory flavors (soy sauce and rock sugar), rich and oily textures, and emphasis on fresh ingredients. However, Shanghai has restaurants from every region of China, so you can find spicy Sichuan, Hunan, and Guizhou cuisine if you prefer heat. International restaurants are also widely available.

Q: Are Shanghai restaurants vegetarian-friendly?

A: Yes, Shanghai has excellent vegetarian options. Traditional Buddhist vegetarian cuisine is a Shanghai specialty (try Fu He Hui, a Michelin-starred vegetarian restaurant). Many international restaurants also offer vegetarian and vegan options. However, some traditional Shanghainese dishes use pork or chicken stock, so inform your server of dietary restrictions. Your driver can help communicate dietary needs in Chinese.

Q: How much should I budget for food in Shanghai?

A: Food costs in Shanghai vary widely: - Street food & local snacks: ¥10–¥50 per dish - Casual local restaurants: ¥50–¥150 per person - Mid-range international restaurants: ¥150–¥400 per person - Fine dining & Michelin-starred: ¥500–¥2,000+ per person For a full-day food tour with multiple stops, budget ¥300–¥800 per person for food, plus the cost of your private car transfer.

Q: Is it safe to eat street food in Shanghai?

A: Yes, street food in Shanghai is generally safe to eat. Look for stalls with high turnover (fresh food), clean preparation areas, and local customers. Avoid stalls that look unclean or have food sitting out for long periods. Popular street foods like jianbing (crepes), chuanr (skewers), and baozi (steamed buns) are generally safe and delicious.

Q: Can I do a food tour at night?

A: Absolutely! Shanghai has a vibrant night food scene. Night markets (like Shouning Road), late-night seafood stalls, and rooftop bars are best experienced after dark. A private car is especially valuable for night food tours for safety and convenience. We recommend an evening tour from 6 PM to 11 PM, with return transfer to your hotel.
